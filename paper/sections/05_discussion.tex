\section{Discussion}

The analyses point to three findings. First, work on running language models at the edge dominates the dataset. The most cited articles, the largest coupling clusters, the foundations of the co-citation network, and two of the three co-word clusters all concern efficient inference, compression, and privacy-preserving training. Second, the business-oriented stream, which covers agents, retrieval, and inventory or supply chain applications, is smaller, less connected to the rest of the field, and unstable in its exact composition. In the co-word map its cluster has the lowest centrality at the main threshold, in the coupling network only three business articles pass the citation threshold (without it they form two small groups), and in the co-citation network its few references sit inside a broad cluster of general reasoning and agent work. Third, a shift is beginning. Between 2025 and 2026, the share of articles with agent, retrieval, and small-business keywords rises, while the share with generic edge keywords falls.

The three streams share concerns such as cost, latency, privacy, and reliability, yet they build on different literatures. The inventory articles found here cite supply chain and operations management work, for example the generative AI studies of \textcite{jackson2024} and \textcite{wamba2023}, and are only weakly linked to the efficient-inference literature: in the co-citation network they fall into a broad background cluster rather than the clusters on compression and edge serving, and in the coupling network only one inventory article passes the threshold. Because the screening and the search strings favour precision (Table~\ref{tab:J}), the claim is not that bridging work does not exist, but that in this dataset it is rare.

The direction of the early shift agrees with an earlier bibliometric review of agentic AI in small, medium, and micro enterprises, which reports a sharp rise since 2022 driven by LLMs and multi-agent systems \parencite{olujimi2025}. The two studies differ in data, since this one uses OpenAlex journal articles from 2023 to 2026 and the earlier one used Scopus, Google Scholar, and grey literature from 2019 to 2025, and they also differ in scope. The agreement is therefore consistent with the earlier finding but does not confirm it. A broad bibliometric review of the LLM literature ended in early 2023 \parencite{fan2024}; the present data start in 2023 and, within the three selected streams, are dominated by edge deployment. That dominance has two possible explanations. The edge literature may simply be larger, or the terms of block B (on-device, edge device) may be more common in titles and abstracts than those of blocks A and C. The sensitivity check does not settle the question, so differences in size between the streams should not be read as a ranking of research importance. A further reason may lie in the questions each stream asks. Edge studies are engineering work on memory, latency, and energy, evaluated on public benchmarks such as those in the first co-citation cluster. Business and inventory studies start from a decision problem, such as demand forecasting or replenishment, and usually use a model without saying where it runs: only one of the 53 articles assigned to the inventory topic states in its abstract that the model runs locally, in that case to keep supply chain data on premise \parencite{wong2026}. The cost and privacy concerns that motivate edge work therefore rarely become design choices in the business stream. This reading is an interpretation of the cluster contents and was not tested statistically.

\subsection{Theoretical Implications}
The maps suggest that research on LLMs for business operations has not yet drawn on the large body of work on efficient and local inference. The field is better described as three loosely connected streams than as one. Studies that join them, for example agent-based inventory support that runs on local models, would fill a gap that the data make visible. Three further claims follow from the labelled clusters. First, the foundations of the field are technical. The two largest co-citation clusters concern edge--cloud serving and model compression, and the agent and supply chain references sit only inside a mixed background cluster, so business applications borrow models from the technical literature but do not yet share its references. Second, privacy appears in both streams with different meanings. In the co-word and coupling maps it is tied to on-device and federated learning (the clusters on privacy-preserving LLMs for IoT and on small language models and on-device AI), whereas in the inventory articles it appears, when at all, as a reason to keep company data on premise \parencite{wong2026}. A shared notion of data control could connect the two streams. Third, the business co-word cluster joins agents with retrieval rather than with local inference. Design questions of business agents, such as tool use, retrieval, and workflow, are thus studied apart from the efficiency questions that decide whether such agents can run on small hardware. These claims describe the structure of the dataset and do not measure the quality or impact of the work.

\subsection{Managerial Implications}
Small businesses that consider an LLM for inventory tasks will find a small and recent body of evidence. In a reading of the abstracts of the 53 articles assigned to the inventory topic, seven evaluated a system on real company or public data, mostly from large operations, for example 18,500 discrepancy records from six distribution centres \parencite{jonnada2026}. Three articles address small businesses directly. In a network of five household-goods stores, about 80\% of items had intermittent demand, and a general chat model guided by structured prompts was used to build a safety stock matrix without direct implementation costs \parencite{cheverda2026}. A chatbot for small grocery stores answered inventory queries with 80\% accuracy using a 7-billion-parameter open model with retrieval from the store database \parencite{burbano2026}. Three practical points follow. First, LLM forecasts for irregular demand vary between runs, so averaging repeated runs or checking against a classical model is advisable \parencite{nakhost2025}. Second, small open models that query the shop's own database are a documented option for stock questions, but their accuracy should be measured on the shop's own records before staff rely on them. Third, running a model locally keeps business data in the shop, but in this dataset no study has tested it in a small business, so early adopters would carry the testing themselves.

\subsection{Limitations}
The study has several limitations, and each points to a direction for further work.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{One database.} The data come from OpenAlex, not Scopus or Web of Science, and metadata quality differs between the databases. For 16.8 million recent publications indexed in all three, OpenAlex has reference counts and internal coverage comparable to Web of Science and Scopus but fewer abstracts \parencite{culbert2025}, which matters for coupling and co-citation and, less, for the keyword-based maps used here. The search should be repeated in Scopus or Web of Science and the maps compared.
  \item \textbf{Incomplete reference data.} Only 282 of the 489 articles (57.7\%) have reference lists, which limits coupling and co-citation. Many of the missing lists come from journals that do not deposit references.
  \item \textbf{Partial year.} The year 2026 is incomplete, so yearly counts and trends for 2026 do not describe a full year.
  \item \textbf{Search sensitivity and breadth.} The search covers titles and abstracts only, and the baseline favours precision. Two wider searches were screened and analysed as sensitivity checks: one of the inventory block (53 added articles, 542 in total) and one of the language-model and device terms (231 added articles, 720 in total). Both kept the separation of the business stream from the edge stream, but the number and boundaries of the clusters changed (see the sensitivity checks in the Results). The records of both checks were screened by language models only.
  \item \textbf{Model-assisted screening.} Screening relied on language models in three passes. In a check of 20 low-confidence second-pass decisions the first author agreed with the model on 8 and rated the other 12, all excluded by the model, as relevant; a third assessment against the written criteria excluded all 12 again and added 4 of the 72 exclusions. Inclusion therefore depends on how strictly a ``main or important part'' is read, and no estimate of the overall accuracy of the screening is available.
  \item \textbf{Keywords.} Keywords come from the automatic assignment in OpenAlex, not from the authors.
  \item \textbf{Unequal blocks.} The three search blocks differ in how common their terms are, so the relative size of the streams partly reflects the search design.
  \item \textbf{Cluster stability and labels.} Co-word membership was stable but modularity was low. The co-citation partition changed with small changes in the data: four added articles merged the small supply chain group into a larger cluster, and the number of clusters ranged from three to five across runs. The coupling clusters exchanged most of their members when one article entered the map. Cluster names were chosen by the first author from model-assisted proposals; for coupling they rest on article titles only.
  \item \textbf{Metadata errors.} Three references whose titles do not fit their citing articles were removed, and other OpenAlex errors may remain. Author, institution, and country data are partly missing; country data are absent for 24\% of the articles.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future Research Avenues}
Four studies follow directly from the gaps found here.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Replication in other databases.} The search blocks, screening criteria, and thresholds should be applied to Scopus and Web of Science, and cluster membership compared with the adjusted Rand index, to test whether the separation of the business stream holds outside OpenAlex.
  \item \textbf{Content analysis of the inventory articles.} The 53 inventory-topic articles should be coded in full text for firm size, data source, model, deployment (cloud or local), and evaluation metric, to establish how far the reported results transfer to small businesses.
  \item \textbf{Field tests of local agents.} An agent-based LLM system for stock checks and reorder suggestions that runs on a local or in-browser model should be tested in small shops on their own transaction data and compared with the shop's current practice and with a cloud model, measuring forecast error, stockouts, response time, and cost.
  \item \textbf{Owners' choices.} A survey or discrete choice experiment with small business owners should measure how they weigh privacy, cost, and accuracy when choosing between local and cloud models, to test whether the privacy argument of the edge literature matters to them.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusions}
This study mapped 489 journal articles from 2023 to 2026 across three streams of LLM research: agents in business settings, models on the device or in the browser, and inventory management. Four findings emerge. Work on edge deployment dominates, since all ten most cited articles, all four coupling clusters, and the main co-cited foundations concern efficient inference, although the definition of the search blocks partly drives this result. The business-oriented stream is small and loosely connected, with the lowest centrality among the co-word clusters, only three business articles in the thresholded coupling map, and only a few co-cited references on generative AI in supply chains, which sit in a broad background cluster. An early shift is visible between 2025 and 2026 towards agents, retrieval, and small enterprises. Finally, the separation of the co-word clusters is stable but loose, while the co-citation partition is unstable and should not be interpreted in detail.

The study contributes a first joint science map of these streams, to the authors' knowledge and within the limits of searches in OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, Crossref, and arXiv, together with a reproducible pipeline that includes stability and sensitivity checks, and a research agenda. For practitioners, the evidence on LLMs for inventory management in small businesses is thin and recent and should be tested on their own data. The agenda has four parts: repeating the search in Scopus and Web of Science, reading the 53 inventory-topic articles in full, testing agent-based inventory systems that run on local or in-browser models in small shops, and asking owners how they weigh privacy, cost, and accuracy.
